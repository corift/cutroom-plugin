# Copywriting for ads

Ad scripts usually miss in one of two ways. The first is that they don't sound like a person talking. They get written to be read and then handed to someone to say, so they come out clipped and clever and sound fake the moment they're spoken. The second is that even when they sound natural, they don't make sense from the customer's side. They describe what the product does instead of what changes for the person watching.

Sections 1 and 2 apply to every ad, whatever the format. Section 3 is about picking a format (a story, a quick before/after, or written copy), because each one is built differently.

The examples below are before-and-after lines from real ad scripts across different products, with names swapped for [name].

## 1. Say it the way a person would say it

The biggest habit to break is writing in fragments. It reads nicely on the page, but nobody talks like that. What makes speech sound like speech is the little words that join thoughts together, like "so", "and", "and then", "because" and "but". Copywriting strips those out, so put them back.

- Written: "Tuesday. 7pm. Empty fridge."
  Said: "It's like seven on a Tuesday and I'm just standing there staring into the fridge."
- Written: "Then she went back to work. No more bookkeeper."
  Said: "But then she went back to work, and all of a sudden I didn't have anyone doing the books anymore."

Lines that read like camera directions have the same problem. If a scene needs setting up, set it up the way people do in conversation, with "so normally when I…" or "say I'm…".

- Written: "6am. Bathroom mirror. Puffy eyes."
  Said: "So I'm usually up at six, and my eyes are always super puffy in the morning."

A few other habits give copy away:

- **A punchline at the end of every paragraph**, like "And that's how I got my mornings back." One in a whole script can work. More than that and the speaker starts sounding like an ad.
- **Neat lists.** "Bills. Appointments. Birthday parties." People's lists are uneven and trail off: "bills, appointments, the after-school stuff, a birthday party like every other weekend…"
- **Numbers the way they're written.** People say "quarter to ten", "like two hundred bucks" and "three forty-two", not "9:47 PM" or "$342.00".

Once all that's fixed, watch for padding. It's easy to overcorrect into something like "and it's basically just an app, or whatever, that you use and all that, so it can actually help you out." It sounds natural, but "or whatever", "and all that" and "so it can actually" add nothing. To decide, take the filler word out and read the line again. If it now sounds like it's being read, put it back. If nothing changed, leave it out.

Finally, use the words the customer would use, not the business word for the same thing. When you're unsure, ask how they'd say it to a friend. Pull words from the brief, customer comments and reviews wherever you can.

- "paperwork" instead of "admin" or "office work"
- "dinner's sorted" instead of "meal planning solution"
- "keeps track of it" or "handles it" instead of "manages" or "processes"
- "it checks with me first" instead of "approval workflow"
- "does the stuff I hate doing" instead of "automates your tasks"
- "I'm not doing it on Sunday night anymore" instead of "saves you time"

## 2. Tell it from the customer's side

Put yourself in the customer's shoes and think about how they'd actually use the product. Once a script already sounds natural, this is usually the fix that matters most.

The common mistake is describing the product's steps. That line may be accurate, but the viewer has no reason to care. Every demo should go from what I used to do, to what I do now, to why that matters to me.

- Product's side: "It uses an algorithm to plan your macros."
  Customer's side: "I used to stand in front of the fridge at seven with no idea what to make. Now the box just shows up with everything for the week, so I don't even have to think about it."
- Product's side: "It reads the receipt and asks which job it's for."
  Customer's side: "Now I just take a picture of the receipt and text it over, so I'm not digging through the truck on Sunday night anymore."

Some other things that keep it on the customer's side:

- **Follow the order they live it in.** Walk through one real day or routine from start to finish, like wake up, then work out, then eat, then how you feel a month later. When a script hops between features, it feels jumbled even if each line is fine.
- **Say what it is the first time you name it**, in a category people already know. "I started using this AI agent called [name]" or "one of those meal kits where the box just shows up" works, while "I started using [name]" leaves them guessing.
- **Keep details from their world, cut details about how it works.** A $35 parking ticket, a receipt stuck in the truck or puffy eyes at 6am make it believable. "Syncs with your CRM", "uses GPT-5" or a long stretch on how the security works make people tune out. If someone reading the script has to stop and ask what a line means, cut it rather than explain it.
- **Say once, plainly, that they stay in control** when the product does things for them. "It never pays anything without asking me first" is enough. Don't invent a worry to answer ("I was nervous at first that…"), and don't oversell it ("the best part is…").
- **One pain per ad.** If the brief names one pain, only talk about that, even if the product does a lot more.
- **Match the real product.** Every number, name and message in the script should match the actual screens. If they don't, change the script.

**If the product talks back** (AI assistants, agents, text-based services, anything you message), two more things help in any format:

- **Give it a name and say it out loud.** Creators name theirs and call it he or she, which does more for "it's like texting a person" than any explanation.
- **Quote what it says back.** The back-and-forth ("want me to pay this with your card?" / "yes, please") is what makes it feel real. Only one side of the conversation reads like a feature list.

## 3. Pick a format

Sections 1 and 2 don't change between formats. What changes is the shape of the script, which hooks fit, and which storytelling moves you use. When writing a batch, mix formats as well as hooks.

### Story

Someone tells a friend what happened: their life before, what changed, and how they use the product now. This format is best when there's a real backstory and the product takes a minute to show.

What makes a story work:

- **Tell it with time markers**, like "so this week", "three days later" or "last Friday", so it plays as something that happened rather than a list of features.
- **React in the moment**, with lines like "that's pretty cool", "are you kidding me?", "no way" or "I don't even remember saving this."
- **Let the doubt come from the speaker or someone close to them**, like a partner asking "wait, is that another one of those apps?" Nobody narrates objection-handling.
- **Admit a flaw**, like "I have the memory of a goldfish", "mom brain" or "I'm good with a saw, bad with paper." It makes the speaker likeable and gives the product a reason to exist.
- **Make the tests harder as you go.** Start with something easy, then something picky, then something it handles that you'd have messed up, and end on something like "okay, you're sold."
- **End small, and make the ask about their problem.** "I'd just try it for a week" or "give it the job you hate most" beats "download now". A quiet last line beats a tagline.

Hooks that fit a story:

- **Something they haven't done in years:** "For ten years I never paid a single bill myself."
- **A real moment, then panic:** a kid asks if a friend can stay for dinner, "yeah of course", then to camera, "I haven't made dinner in years."
- **Nobody else will do it:** they ask their partner, mom and kid to help and each one says no, so they try the product.
- **Someone close is skeptical:** a partner overhears them and asks "wait, what is that?"
- **The help that went away:** they used to have someone handling it, then that person was gone.

The order a strong story follows, shown for two different products. Borrow the order, not the words.

| What happens | Bill-pay assistant | Meal kit |
|---|---|---|
| Something they never had to deal with | "For ten years, I never paid a single bill myself." | "For years my husband did all the cooking." |
| Then that changed | "Then I sold the company, and I didn't have an assistant anymore." | "And then he started working nights, so all of a sudden dinner was on me." |
| What they started using, in words people know | "So I started using this AI agent. It's not a chatbot, it actually does stuff." | "So I started getting one of those meal kits where the box just shows up." |
| The first thing they use it for, and what changed | Snapped a parking ticket, it asked if it should pay it, "yes, please." | "It comes with everything already portioned, so I'm not running to the store at six." |
| The next thing in the same routine | A lawyer's invoice came in, and saying yes was all they did. | "And the recipes are like twenty minutes, so we're actually eating before eight." |
| That they're still in control, said once | "It never pays anything without asking me." | "And I pick the meals every week, so it's stuff the kids will actually eat." |
| Who it's for, and a small ask | "If you run a business or a house, give it the job you hate most." | "So if dinner's on you now too, I'd just try it for a week." |

### Quick before/after

"I used to spend X hours on this, and now I just…" There's no backstory. It gets to the point in the first line and is done in about twenty seconds. This format is best for short spots, when you're testing lots of audiences fast, or when the pain is easy to put a number on. The same skeleton works for almost any audience: you swap the chore, the number and the two examples.

The skeleton:

1. **The time they used to lose, and where:** "So I used to spend like ten hours every single week at my computer, late at night, typing my invoices into Excel."
2. **The switch:** "But now I just text [name]."
3. **What it is, in one line:** "It's an AI agent that helps you get done the stuff you just don't want to do."
4. **Where they are instead:** "So instead of going home and getting on my computer every night, I just text it while I'm on the job site."
5. **Two examples from their day:** "So if I need an invoice for a job, or I need to follow up with a client who hasn't paid, I just text [name]."
6. **Control, said once:** "It knows how I like things done, and it always checks with me first, just in case."
7. **A small ask:** "It's free right now, so I'd just give it the thing you hate doing the most."

Here's the same skeleton for a different product (a meal kit):

> So I used to spend like three hours every Sunday figuring out what we're eating that week, making a list and then fighting everyone at the grocery store. But now the box just shows up. It's one of those meal kits where everything comes already portioned. So instead of spending my Sunday at the store, I'm actually home with the kids. So on a Tuesday when I get in at six, I just grab a bag and it's like twenty minutes, and the kids actually eat it. And I still pick the meals every week, so it's stuff we like. I'd just try it for a week and see if you get your Sunday back.

Things to watch in this format:

- **The number is the whole hook, so make it sound like an estimate.** "Like ten hours a week" sounds like a person. "10+ hours weekly" sounds like a stat from a landing page.
- **Keep it from turning into a feature list.** With no story around it, it's easy to drift into "it has access to all your tools and it can do any task." One line about what it can reach is fine. After that, stay with the two examples from their day.
- **Put the where-they-are-instead line in.** "While I'm on the job site" or "I'm actually home with the kids" is the payoff. Without it, the script is just a list of tasks.

Hooks that fit a quick before/after:

- **The hours you used to lose:** "I used to spend twelve hours every week chasing my team about how our ads were doing."
- **The overwhelmed list:** "I feel like I'm holding like forty things in my head right now…"

### Written copy (Meta primary text, captions)

The same ideas apply. Write it the way the person would text a friend about it, not the way a brand describes itself. It can be shorter than a spoken script, but it's still full sentences with the connecting words left in.

- Brand: "Dinner, simplified. Pre-portioned ingredients and 20-minute recipes, delivered."
- Person: "Dinner's been on me since my husband started working nights, and honestly the box showing up with everything already portioned is the only reason we're eating before eight."

## 4. How to work on copy with the user

- **Start from scripts that already work.** Before writing a spoken ad, transcribe 6–8 top organic TikToks in the category and note each one's hook, shape and views. Borrow their shapes, not their words. On Northern Fitness, this beat the version written from the brand's ads alone. What the top videos shared:
  - The first sentence names the stake: a budget, "the ultimate…", or money you're losing.
  - There's one real price or detail per item.
  - Nobody says "buy now".

- **Get a voice note first.** Have the user read the script out loud or record how they'd say it. One read-through usually gets further than several rewrites. Then use their transcript as the script. Only remove false starts, repeated words and obvious transcription errors. Don't swap their words for "better" ones, don't reorder, and don't add lines. If something is missing, like an ending or the offer, suggest it separately and label it as an addition.
- **Apply line edits as they gave them**, and only smooth where the lines join. Polishing their wording back into something tidier is how the copywriting habits creep back in.
- **Read it out loud before handing it over.** Every line should be something the character would say in one breath without sounding like they're reading, it should follow on from the line before, and you should be able to say what problem it solves for the person watching. If it fails any of those, fix it or cut it.
- **Make variants actually different**, with a different format or hook from section 3, not the same lines shuffled around.
- **Check the brief** for banned words and required phrasing, and follow them exactly.
